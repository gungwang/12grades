# Are US College Students "Getting Less Smart"? What NAEP, PISA, and College-Readiness Data Actually Show

> **Scope & method:** This note was written to answer an outside question about US education ("why are US college students less and less smart in the past 5 years?") and is unrelated to this repository's Chinese K-12 curriculum content. It is kept here as a one-off, citable research memo, following the docs/ convention of dated, source-linked notes (see `standards-mapping.md`, `moe2025-alignment.md`). All figures below were read directly from the primary source pages/PDFs listed in [§8 Sources](#8-sources) on **2026-10-28**; numbers are quoted or recomputed from official tables, not taken from secondary summaries. Where a figure could not be verified against a primary source in the time available, that gap is stated explicitly rather than filled in from memory.

## Bottom line

No federal, OECD, or college-testing body measures the "smartness" (intelligence) of enrolled US college students, so the premise as stated cannot be confirmed or refuted directly — that data simply does not exist (see [§5](#5-is-there-direct-evidence-about-enrolled-college-students) and [§7](#7-limits-and-uncertainties)). What *is* well documented is narrower: since the last pre-pandemic measurements (2019), **academic-achievement** scores (not IQ) for the K-12 cohorts who are now entering or recently entered college have fallen on most national reading/math measures, recovered only partially, and declined further for the oldest tested cohorts (grade 12, 13-year-olds) in the newest 2023-2024 data. Declines are concentrated among already lower-performing students, so average scores understate how uneven the change has been. Measures closer to the college gate (ACT, SAT) also show lower average scores over the last five years, but both are confounded by large shifts in which students take each test, in opposite directions, which materially muddies any interpretation of "ability" trends.

## 1. Two conflations in the question

1. **Achievement ≠ intelligence.** NAEP, PISA, the SAT, and the ACT are curriculum-linked **achievement** tests: they measure learned knowledge and skills in reading, math, and science against a specific test framework ([NAEP reading framework](https://www.nagb.gov/naep-subject-areas/reading.html); [PISA frameworks](https://www.oecd.org/content/dam/oecd/en/publications/reports/2026/05/pisa-2025-assessment-and-analytical-framework_3abce6d6/86c36975-en.pdf)). None of them is an IQ/general-cognitive-ability test, and no source found in this research tracks IQ trends for US college students specifically. This note is about achievement, not intelligence, because that is what the official sources measure.
2. **College students ≠ K-12/15-year-olds.** NAEP's main assessments cover grades 4, 8, and 12; NAEP's long-term-trend (LTT) assessments cover ages 9, 13, and 17; PISA covers 15-year-olds nearing the end of compulsory schooling ([nces.ed.gov/surveys/pisa](https://nces.ed.gov/surveys/pisa/)). **None of these test enrolled college students.** The closest official link to "college readiness" is (a) grade-12 NAEP, which the National Assessment Governing Board's own 2008-2014 research program validated as an indicator of academic preparedness for college ([NAGB preparedness research](https://www.nagb.gov/naep/postsecondary-preparedness/preparedness-research.html)), and (b) the ACT/SAT, taken by graduating high-school seniors before they enroll. Sections 2-4 below cover K-12/15-year-olds; Section 5 covers what little exists on enrolled college students themselves.

## 2. K-12 achievement: NAEP main assessment (grades 4, 8, 12)

Source: The Nation's Report Card (NCES), 2024 cycle, retrieved 2026-10-28. The most recent main NAEP reading and mathematics assessment was given in **2024** to ~117,400 grade-4, ~114,600 grade-8, and ~24,300 grade-12 students in reading, and ~117,900 / 115,200 / 19,300 respectively in math ([reading overview](https://nces.ed.gov/nationsreportcard/reading/); [mathematics overview](https://nces.ed.gov/nationsreportcard/mathematics/)).

| Subject / grade | 2024 vs. 2022 | 2024 vs. 2019 (pre-pandemic) | 2024 vs. earliest baseline | Source |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Reading, grade 4 | **-2 points**; 31% at/above *Proficient* (-2 pts) | **-5 points**; -4 pts *Proficient* | not significantly different from 1992 | [report](https://www.nationsreportcard.gov/reports/reading/2024/g4_8/?grade=4) |
| Reading, grade 8 | lower (qualitative only — point value not available from the server-rendered page) | n/a in source text | — | [about page](https://nces.ed.gov/nationsreportcard/reading/) |
| Reading, grade 12 (first grade-12 reading test since 2019) | — | **-3 points** | **-10 points vs. 1992** | [report](https://www.nationsreportcard.gov/reports/reading/2024/g12/) |
| Math, grade 4 | **+2 points** (improved); 39% *Proficient* (+3 pts) | **-3 points**; -2 pts *Proficient*; below-*Basic* share +5 pts | higher than 1990 | [report](https://www.nationsreportcard.gov/reports/mathematics/2024/g4_8/?grade=4) |
| Math, grade 8 | not significantly different | n/a in source text | — | [about page](https://nces.ed.gov/nationsreportcard/mathematics/) |
| Math, grade 12 (first grade-12 math test since 2019) | — | **-3 points** | **-3 points vs. 2005** (framework changed in 2005) | [report](https://www.nationsreportcard.gov/reports/mathematics/2024/g12/) |

**Important gap:** grade-12 NAEP — the cohort closest to "about to enter college" — was **not administered at all between 2019 and 2024**, a 5-year blackout that covers almost exactly the period the original question asks about ([nationsreportcard.gov/reading/](https://www.nationsreportcard.gov/reading/), pre-2024 cache: "reading assessment at grade 12 was last administered nationally in 2019"). The picture is therefore mixed, not uniformly negative: grade-4 math partly rebounded by 2024, grade-8 math was flat, while reading at every grade and grade-12 math kept falling relative to 2019.

## 3. K-12 achievement: NAEP long-term trend (ages 9, 13, 17)

LTT uses a different, decades-old item pool sampled by **age** rather than grade, and NCES states LTT and main-NAEP results "cannot be directly compared" to each other ([nationsreportcard.gov/ltt](https://www.nationsreportcard.gov/ltt/)).

- **Age 9** (tested Jan-Mar 2022, reported as "2022"): reading +7 points vs. the 1971 baseline but **-5 points vs. 2020**; math +15 points vs. 1973 but **-7 points vs. 2020** ([LTT overview](https://www.nationsreportcard.gov/ltt/)).
- **Age 13** (tested Oct-Dec 2022-23, reported as "2023"): reading **-4 points vs. 2020** (-7 vs. a decade earlier); math **-9 points vs. 2020** (-14 vs. a decade earlier) — the largest age-13 math drop in the LTT series ([2023 LTT highlights](https://www.nationsreportcard.gov/highlights/ltt/2023/)).
  - Declines were **not evenly spread**: in math, the 10th/25th-percentile (lowest-performing) students fell 12-14 points vs. 2020 versus 6-8 points for 50th/75th/90th-percentile students — a statistically significant widening of the gap between weaker and stronger students. The White-Black score gap widened from 35 to 42 points, and the male-female gap widened as well (female -11 vs. male -7).
- **Age 17**: the most recent available LTT result is from **2012** — no newer age-17 LTT data exists ([NCES 2012 report](https://nces.ed.gov/nationsreportcard/pubs/main2012/2013456.aspx), linked from [nationsreportcard.gov/ltt](https://www.nationsreportcard.gov/ltt/)). This is the age group closest to incoming college freshmen, and NCES simply has no post-2012 trend line for it.

## 4. 15-year-olds internationally: PISA 2025

PISA is OECD-coordinated and **administered in the US by NCES**, which is the first-party source used here (OECD's own site returned HTTP 403 to automated fetches during this research). The PISA 2025 US results were released **September 2026** ([nces.ed.gov/surveys/pisa/pisa2025](https://nces.ed.gov/surveys/pisa/pisa2025/index.asp)); 91 education systems participated, 38 of them OECD members.

| Domain (2025 role) | US mean (2025) | vs. OECD average | vs. most recent cycles | Long-run change | 90th-10th pct. gap |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Reading (minor domain\*) | 490 | above (461); tops 75 of 89 other systems | **lower than 2018 and 2022** | not sig. different from 2000 | **312 pts — the widest of any participating system** (OECD avg. 264); low performers worse than in *every* earlier cycle |
| Mathematics (minor domain\*) | 463 | not significantly different (tied with most of the 38 OECD members: lower than 14, higher than 7) | **not significantly different from 2022** | **-20 pts vs. 2003** | 260 pts (OECD avg. not stated as different) |
| Science (major domain\*) | 502 | above (482); tops 70 of 90 other systems | not significantly different from 2022 | not significantly different from 2006 (earliest comparable cycle) | 298 pts, wider than 78 other systems; gap **widened vs. 2015** (top scored higher, bottom scored lower) |

\* PISA rotates which domain gets the fullest, newest item set ("major domain") each cycle; the other two are "minor domains" tested only with older trend items. In 2025, science was the major domain and reading/math were minor — a methodological reason not to over-read small 2025 reading/math shifts. Sources: [reading](https://nces.ed.gov/surveys/pisa/pisa2025/inc/html/reading/main.asp), [mathematics](https://nces.ed.gov/surveys/pisa/pisa2025/inc/html/mathematics/main.asp), [science](https://nces.ed.gov/surveys/pisa/pisa2025/inc/html/science/main.asp), [overview](https://nces.ed.gov/surveys/pisa/pisa2025/inc/html/home/home.asp).

Takeaway: the US is still mid-to-high internationally (above the OECD average in reading and science), which cuts against a narrative of broad relative collapse — but math is roughly at the OECD average rather than above it, is 20 points below its own 2003 level, and in every domain the gap between the strongest and weakest US 15-year-olds is unusually wide by international standards.

## 5. The college-entry boundary: ACT and SAT

These are taken by high-school seniors, i.e., **before** college, and both are compiled in NCES's *Digest of Education Statistics* 2023 edition (tables 226.10, 226.50, 226.60 — prepared Nov-Dec 2023, the most recent edition of these specific tables as of this research), sourced to ACT's and the College Board's own annual reports. I downloaded the underlying NCES Excel workbooks and College Board's own 2025 PDF report directly to verify figures.

**ACT national composite score** ([NCES table 226.50](https://nces.ed.gov/programs/digest/d23/tables/xls/tabn226.50.xlsx), source: ACT *High School Profile Report*, 1995-2023):

| Year | 2018 | 2019 | 2020 | 2021 | 2022 | 2023 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Composite (1-36 scale) | 20.8 | 20.7 | 20.6 | 20.3 | 19.8 | 19.5 |
| % of all US grads tested | 51.5% | 47.7% | 45.1% | 34.6% | 36.0% | 37.1% |

Every subject score fell too (English 20.2→18.6, Math 20.5→19.0, Reading 21.3→20.1, Science 20.7→19.6, 2018→2023). But the test-taking **population fell by more than a third** relative to its 2016 peak (57.9% of graduates tested, down to 37.1% by 2023) as several states dropped ACT-for-all mandates ([table 226.60](https://nces.ed.gov/programs/digest/d23/tables/xls/tabn226.60.xlsx)), so year-to-year comparisons mix a real performance change with a changing, more self-selected pool of test-takers. State-level data make the confound concrete: California's ACT-taking share collapsed from 27% (2018) to 4% (2023) while its *composite rose* from 22.7 to 25.7 — the opposite direction from the national trend, driven by who was left taking the test. (I could not locate ACT's 2024/2025 national composite via a direct, first-party URL in the time available — see [§7](#7-limits-and-uncertainties).)

**SAT Total Group mean score** ([NCES table 226.10](https://nces.ed.gov/programs/digest/d23/tables/xls/tabn226.10.xlsx) for 2017-2023, source: College Board *Total Group SAT Suite Annual Reports*; 2025 figures read directly from College Board's [2025 Total Group report PDF](https://reports.collegeboard.org/media/pdf/2025-total-group-sat-suite-of-assessments-annual-report.pdf)):

| Year | 2017 | 2021 | 2022 | 2023 | 2025 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Total mean (400-1600 scale) | 1060 | 1060 | 1050 | 1028 | 1029 |
| Evidence-Based Reading & Writing mean | 533 | 533 | 529 | 520 | 521 |
| Math mean | 527 | 528 | 521 | 508 | 508 |
| Test-takers (class of) | 1.72M | 1.51M | 1.74M | 1.91M | 2.00M |

Unlike the ACT, the SAT-taking population **grew** (roughly +33% from 2021 to 2025) as more states added SAT School Day mandates — a compositional shift in the *opposite* direction from the ACT's shrinking pool, which again complicates reading the score drop as a pure ability change. Separately, College Board switched from a paper to a shorter, adaptive **digital SAT** starting with the class of 2024; its own 2025 report states cross-test/subscore metrics "are not calculated for the digital SAT" and cautions that "the current SAT Suite benchmark outcomes should not be compared against results from the old SAT…, as the score scale and benchmark definitions have changed" (2025 Total Group report, pp. 2-3). Of the 2025 graduating class's SAT takers, 39% met both College and Career Readiness Benchmarks (64% met the reading/writing benchmark alone, 41% the math benchmark alone; p.4).

## 6. Is there direct evidence about enrolled college students?

Largely no, at the national level:

- No federal or OECD body gives a NAEP/PISA-style, repeated, nationally representative achievement (let alone intelligence) test to students **after** they enroll in college. NCES's postsecondary systems (IPEDS, NPSAS) track enrollment, cost, aid, and completion — not learning outcomes.
- The nearest instrument, CAE's **Collegiate Learning Assessment (CLA+)**, is opt-in by institution and used mainly for internal/institutional benchmarking; CAE's own materials do not publish a NAEP-style national trend line ([cae.org CLA+ FAQ](https://cae.org/resources/cla-frequently-asked-questions-2/)). This is a genuine evidence gap, not a "no change found" result.
- What *is* tracked nationally is enrollment, not learning: undergraduate enrollment, which fell sharply in 2020-2021, stood at **15.5 million in spring 2026, up 1.3% year-over-year**, per the National Student Clearinghouse Research Center's enrollment-insights series ([nscresearchcenter.org](https://nscresearchcenter.org/)) — i.e., the college-going population is growing again, not narrowing to a smaller, more elite group, which would be one obvious explanation for falling test averages but is not what the enrollment data show.

## 7. What might explain the K-12/entry-level declines (sources' own words, with caveats)

NCES is explicit that NAEP is **not designed to identify causes**: "Survey questionnaire results… do not establish a cause-and-effect relationship between the characteristic or experience and student achievement. NAEP is not designed to identify the causes of performance differences. Numerous factors interact to influence student achievement…" ([2023 LTT highlights](https://www.nationsreportcard.gov/highlights/ltt/2023/)). With that caveat, NCES's own age-13 survey data show two correlates, both measured in the same assessment:

- **Chronic absenteeism roughly doubled.** The share of 13-year-olds reporting 5+ missed school days in the prior month rose from **5% (2020) to 10% (2023)**; students with fewer missed days scored higher on average (correlational only).
- **Recreational reading fell to a record low.** Only **14%** of 13-year-olds reported reading for fun "almost every day" in 2023, down from 17% in 2020 and 27% in 2012 — the lowest share since this question has been asked; daily readers scored higher on average (again, correlational only).

Beyond these two NCES-documented correlates, the 2020-2022 score drops across NAEP and PISA line up in time with COVID-19-era school closures and remote instruction, which is the most commonly cited contextual explanation in the source material reviewed here, though none of the primary sources in this note formally establishes that causal chain. Other popular explanations (grade inflation, phone/social-media use, teacher shortages, changes in reading instruction method) were not verified against a primary source in this research and are not asserted here; they should be treated as open hypotheses, not findings of this note.

## 8. Limits and uncertainties

1. **No measure of "smartness"/intelligence exists for US college students** in any source checked; this note can only speak to curriculum-linked achievement in reading, math, and science.
2. **No national learning measure of enrolled college students exists at all** (§5); every "college" data point here is either about high-school seniors who have not yet enrolled (NAEP grade 12, ACT, SAT) or about headcounts (NSC), not demonstrated learning of current undergraduates.
3. **Grade-12 main NAEP has a 2019-2024 gap**, and **age-17 LTT has not been updated since 2012** — the two NAEP cohorts closest to "about to start college" have the thinnest recent data of all.
4. **ACT and SAT test-taker populations moved in opposite directions**: the ACT-tested share of US graduates fell from 57.9% (2016) to 37.1% (2023) -- a 20.8 percentage-point drop, about 36% in relative terms -- while the SAT-tested class grew by roughly a third (2021 to 2025). Neither trend can be cleanly read as a pure ability change without further adjustment that is beyond the scope of this note.
5. I could not retrieve a 2024 or 2025 **national ACT composite score** from a first-party ACT or NCES URL in the time available (ACT's current site search and sitemap did not surface a reachable national-report PDF for those years); this is a cited gap, not an inferred number.
6. PISA 2025 reading and math were tested as **minor domains** (older, smaller item sets) while science was the **major domain**, which NCES flags as a reason for caution when comparing across domains within the same cycle.
7. Both the ACT and the SAT changed test design within the period discussed (digital, adaptive SAT since the class of 2024; ACT made the science section optional — confirmed on [act.org](https://www.act.org/) ("science and writing optional")) — another reason to treat the most recent 1-2 years of each series as not fully comparable in form to earlier years in the same five-year window.
8. Figures above are quoted or directly recomputed from the cited primary tables/PDFs; absolute NAEP/PISA scale scores (e.g., "285 out of 500") were not extractable from the JavaScript-rendered chart pages used by nationsreportcard.gov and nces.ed.gov's PISA 2025 site, so this note reports the point differences and percentages that NCES states in its own text rather than estimating the underlying scale scores.
