# Broad-access college students: what lower-percentile data can show

**Research date:** 2026-10-28  
**Question:** Are typical students at non-elite U.S. colleges becoming less capable, after setting aside the top 10–20% of performers?

## Bottom line

There is not enough public, representative evidence to establish a 2021–2026 trend in learning among undergraduates at broad-access U.S. colleges. The main federal postsecondary data system, IPEDS, receives institution-level aggregates rather than student-level learning results ([NCES, IPEDS survey components](https://nces.ed.gov/ipeds/survey-components)). Some colleges use direct assessments such as CLA+, but the public materials reviewed do not provide a recurring, nationally representative learning trend broken out by broad-access institution type and student percentile ([CAE, CLA+ FAQ](https://cae.org/resources/cla-frequently-asked-questions-2/)).

The strongest available percentile evidence is from **K–12 students who may later enter college**, not enrolled undergraduates. It shows some declines below the top decile, especially in reading and mathematics, but the pattern varies by age, subject, and comparison year. It cannot establish that general-college students are less intelligent or are learning less.

## Definitions and limits

- **“Top 10–20%” refers to student test-score percentiles, not colleges.** This memo focuses on the 10th, 25th, 50th, and 75th percentiles and uses the 90th percentile as an upper-tail comparison. Public summaries do not report an exact 80th-percentile score or a mean recalculated after removing the top 10% or 20%; a percentile point is not an average for everyone below it.
- **“Broad-access/general college” is an institutional category, not a student score.** A practical core group for future analysis is public two-year/associate-serving and open-admission colleges, with less-selective public four-year institutions analyzed separately. IPEDS provides institution-grouping variables such as control, level, sector, and Carnegie classification; “general college” is not a single federal category ([NCES, IPEDS institutional groupings](https://nces.ed.gov/ipeds/use-the-data/institutional-groupings-in-ipeds)).
- **The K–12 results below are pipeline context only.** NAEP and PISA test school-age students, not enrolled college students. Their population percentiles are cross-sectional; they do not track the same individuals or measure intelligence.

These results do **not** show that top performers are “always” unchanged: for example, the age-13 NAEP 2020–2023 scores declined even at the 90th percentile. Conversely, in grade-12 NAEP and some PISA comparisons, the 90th percentile was stable while lower percentiles fell. This describes changes in score distributions across cohorts—not changes in the intelligence of the same individuals.

## What can and cannot be concluded

1. **For enrolled students at broad-access colleges:** the public sources reviewed do not establish whether learning among the median or lower 80–90% improved or declined from 2021 to 2026. A direct, repeated, representative undergraduate assessment broken down by institutional access and score percentile was not found.
2. **For the feeder pipeline:** several recent school-age measures show weaker reading or mathematics performance below the upper tail. That is a legitimate preparation concern, but it is not evidence that current undergraduates at non-elite colleges are less intelligent.
3. **For causes:** do not infer a single cause from these score trends. NCES explicitly cautions that NAEP survey associations do not establish cause and effect and that NAEP is not designed to identify causes ([NCES 2023 highlights](https://www.nationsreportcard.gov/highlights/ltt/2023/)).
